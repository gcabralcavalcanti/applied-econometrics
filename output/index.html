01 · Is there a Phillips-curve trade-off in Brazil? (2012-2026)

Final report (PDF) · Code and method

Question

Does higher unemployment go with lower inflation in Brazil, once inflation inertia and expectations are in the model?

Result

  • In the static model the unemployment coefficient is -0.002 (SE 0.010), with no stars: no static trade-off.
  • In the dynamic model, lagged inflation has a coefficient of 0.398 and the Focus expectation 0.686, both significant at 1%; unemployment stays insignificant at 0.007.
  • R² rises from 0.000 to 0.421, on 169 monthly observations.
  • H0: γ + δ = 1 is not rejected (p = 0.432): the data do not reject a vertical long-run Phillips curve.
  • With Newey-West errors the standard error on the expectation term rises from 0.111 to 0.177; it stays significant.
  • With seasonal dummies the expectation coefficient is 0.783 and R² 0.523; without the COVID months unemployment is 0.001.

Reading: Brazilian inflation is driven by inertia and expectations; labour-market slack barely shows up. Descriptive, not causal: expectations are endogenous and the model uses the unemployment rate, not the gap to its natural rate.

02 · Crime and deterrence: a county panel

Final report (PDF) · Code and method

Question

Do counties with a higher probability of arrest, conviction and prison have less crime, and how much of the answer changes once fixed county traits, common year shocks and serial correlation are handled?

Result

Specificationlog P(arrest)log police per capita
Pooled OLS-0.6330.352
County fixed effects-0.3950.422
Two-way fixed effects-0.3600.422
  • Breusch-Pagan LM = 191.60 and White LM = 410.04, both with p < 0.0001: heteroskedasticity is clear.
  • In the two-way model, clustering by county raises the standard error on the arrest elasticity from 0.033 to 0.064.
  • On police per capita the clustered standard error is 0.093, against 0.027 before. Both coefficients keep three stars after clustering.
  • Fixed effects cut the pooled arrest elasticity by about a third, so part of the pooled deterrence was county traits.

Reading: higher arrest probability goes with less crime even within a county. The positive police coefficient is the classic simultaneity sign (counties hire police where crime is high), so none of this is a causal estimate.

03 · Evangelical density and recorded domestic violence in Brazilian municipalities

Final report (PDF) · Code and method

Question

Do municipalities with a larger share of evangelicals record more (or less) domestic violence against women?

Result

  • With state fixed effects and HC1 errors, the evangelical-density coefficient is 0.0009 (SE 0.0009, p = 0.325), on 5046 municipalities.
  • Adding the non-domestic notification proxy raises R² from 0.171 to 0.439; the proxy coefficient is 0.4237, significant at 1%, and evangelical density stays at 0.0009.
  • The Poisson model with a population offset gives 0.0080, an incidence-rate ratio of 1.0081, not significant.
  • In WLS the coefficient is 0.0019, not significant.
  • Across the six robustness columns, the coefficient reaches significance only once: at 10%, with errors clustered by state (0.0009*).
  • With the 3-year denominator the coefficient is 0.0007 (p = 0.414).

Reading: no robust association. What explains recorded rates is how much a municipality notifies, not its religious composition. Associational, not causal; the denominator is the whole population, not women at risk.

04 · Picking funds from a retail menu: does past performance help, and what do fees cost?

Final report (PDF) · Code and method

Question

An investment committee must choose which funds to offer on a menu (a company savings plan, a platform shortlist). Two questions decide the rule: do last year's winners keep winning, and do cheaper funds leave investors better off?

Result

  • Last year's best fifth of equity funds beat the worst fifth by 1.11 points the following year, with a standard error of 4.61: no usable signal.
  • The slope of next-year on this-year excess return is 0.094 for equity and 0.182 for fixed income, both significant but small.
  • In fixed income, 39.3% of the best quintile stays best the next year, against 20% by chance; in equity, 28.0%.
  • Each extra point of expense ratio goes with 0.735 points less net excess return in equity and 0.380 in multi-asset, both significant at 1%; in fixed income the slope is 0.061 and not significant.
  • The cheapest third of equity funds (median expense ratio 0.16%) returned -4.99 points over the CDI the next year, against -7.18 for the most expensive third (median 2.82%).
  • Over 10 years, the median equity investor who picks from the cheapest third ends with 0.72 of what the CDI would have given, against 0.69 for a random pick and 0.66 for last year's best quintile.
  • Picking from the cheapest third and skipping last year's worst quintile gives the best median in every class: 0.74 in equity, 0.88 in multi-asset and 0.96 in fixed income.

Reading: past returns barely help; fees do. A menu rule of "cheapest third within each class, minus last year's worst" beats picking at random or chasing winners, but in 2021-2025 even that trailed the CDI. Four year pairs is a short history: these are associations in one period, not a law.
